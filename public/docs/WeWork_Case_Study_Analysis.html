Business question

WeWork went from one of the most highly valued private companies in the world to filing for bankruptcy in under five years

What did the financial, operational, and corporate governance data reveal about the sustainability of the business model before the collapse?

To answer this question, financial information, operating metrics, capital structure, long-term commitments, and corporate decisions were analyzed.

Analytical framework

The case was analyzed across four dimensions:

01 · Financial performance

Revenue, net losses, and cost structure.

02 · Financial structure

Liabilities, equity, debt, cash, and lease commitments.

03 · Business model

Long-term lease agreements, flexible memberships, and occupancy.

04 · Corporate decisions

Expansion strategy, capital allocation, control structure, related-party transactions, and management incentives.

Context

WeWork's valuation reached approximately USD 47.0B in January 2019, four years later, the company filed for bankruptcy. This valuation reflected very high expectations of future growth. However, its financial statements showed a cost and obligation structure that made it necessary to analyze how much it actually cost WeWork to sustain that growth.

Figure 1. WeWork valuation, 2016–2023. Source: funding rounds and SEC filings.

1. Revenue growth did not translate into profitability

WeWork's revenue grew significantly as the company expanded its operations, but this growth was not accompanied by an improvement in profitability. Between 2017 and 2022, revenue increased while the company maintained substantial net losses, revealing a gap between the growth of the business and its ability to generate sustainable results.

Figure 2. Revenue and net loss, 2017–2022. Source: WeWork S-1 Filing (SEC) and StockAnalysis.

Revenue growth was accompanied by losses and a high cost structure. As a result, the growth of the business did not necessarily translate into an improvement in profitability or in WeWork's financial position.

2. The business model had a cost structure that was difficult to sustain

WeWork's model had one fundamental characteristic: it took on long-term lease commitments, while its revenue depended on more flexible membership contracts and on maintaining high occupancy levels. While demand and occupancy were growing, this structure could support expansion; however, when growth slowed, a significant portion of costs remained, increasing pressure on the operation.

Adding to this was a high concentration of control in its founder and CEO, Adam Neumann, who held voting rights far greater than those of other shareholders. This reduced the checks on expansion decisions and on the new lease commitments assumed by the company.

3. Growth increased financial obligations

WeWork's expansion was accompanied by strong growth in its liabilities and a deterioration of its equity. As the company added new locations and took on commitments to sustain its expansion, its financial obligations also grew.

Figure 3. Total liabilities and equity, 2017–2019. Source: WeWork S-1 Filing (SEC).

As of June 2019, WeWork held approximately USD 2.47B in cash, while its liabilities reached USD 24.64B and its equity was negative. The gap between these figures shows that the growth of the operation came with an increasingly demanding financial structure, with obligations that far exceeded available resources and equity that was already in deficit.

Corporate decisions

Financial results were also shaped by three aspects of WeWork's management and structure.

01

Expansion strategy

WeWork pursued an accelerated expansion strategy, rapidly growing its footprint and taking on commitments for new locations. This pace of growth implied greater capital needs, operating costs, and financial obligations.

02

Concentration of control

The share structure established different voting rights depending on share class. Class B shares carried 20 votes per share, compared to 1 vote per share for Class A. This structure concentrated decision-making power among certain shareholders and created a significant gap between economic ownership and voting power.

03

Related-party transactions

Documents filed by WeWork with the SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) recorded several transactions related to Neumann. These included a loan of approximately USD 362M associated with the exercise of stock options, a payment of approximately USD 5.9M for the acquisition of rights to the "We" trademark, which belonged to Neumann, and lease transactions tied to properties in which he held an interest.

Between December 2017 and June 2019, workstations grew from 214,000 to 604,000 and memberships rose from 186,000 to 527,000. Over the same period, the share of enterprise memberships (companies with more than 500 employees) increased from 28% to 40%, indicating a growing share of corporate clients within WeWork's membership base. Together, these indicators show both the growth in the scale of the business and the shift in the composition of its customers.

2. Operating performance

Among the metrics used by WeWork was Community Adjusted EBITDA, a metric used by WeWork to measure the operating performance of its workspaces, excluding corporate expenses, opening costs, and other expenses the company considered non-representative of operating performance.

Figure 4. Community Adjusted EBITDA and net loss, H1 2019. Source: WeWork S-1 Filing (SEC).

The result was negative even under this adjusted measure, meaning that growth in members and capacity did not, on its own, translate into a positive operating result.

Conclusion

WeWork's business model was not financially sustainable: operating costs were high, the company grew without achieving profitability, and it accumulated high levels of debt and financial commitments. Adding to this was a high concentration of decision-making power in its founder and CEO, along with corporate decisions that increased the risks associated with the lack of profitability. Against this backdrop, the USD 47.0B valuation reflected growth expectations that were not consistent with the financial and operating capacity the company actually showed.
